Where Aionik sits in chip fabrication

There are a handful of established ways to make a microfluidic chip. Each is good at something and each breaks down somewhere, and the pattern in where they break is the reason this company exists. This page sets out the landscape at the level of what each method can and cannot do.

PDMS soft lithography

Cast silicone against a photolithographic master mold. The academic standard for two decades, and the route most published microfluidics is built on.

Strengths

  • Optically clear and gas permeable, which suits live cell work
  • A mold is reusable, so the cost of the master is amortized across castings
  • Enormous published body of protocols and known geometry
  • Fine feature resolution, set by photolithography rather than by the casting

Limits

  • The master needs a cleanroom, which is a large fixed cost carried by one step
  • Every chip after the mold is hand work: pouring, degassing, punching, bonding
  • The plasma bond is a single attempt inside a window of roughly 15 minutes to an hour
  • Absorbs small hydrophobic molecules, which distorts drug and assay work
  • Adsorbs proteins onto the channel surface, which affects cell adhesion and depletes media components
  • Uncrosslinked oligomers leach out into the culture medium, so the material is not inert in a long running organoid experiment
  • Does not scale to production, and the seam remains the weak point

Hot embossing

A thermoplastic sheet heated just above its glass transition and pressed against a mold insert, then cooled and demolded. The middle route between soft lithography and injection molding.

Strengths

  • Tooling runs roughly $5,000 to $50,000, an order of magnitude below injection molding
  • Labs often improvise with a heated laboratory press costing a few thousand dollars, but commercial hot embossing and wafer bonding systems are sold on a quote basis, estimated at $150,000 to $350,000
  • Materials such as COC absorb far less than PDMS
  • Micron scale features with good replication fidelity
  • Economic from roughly a thousand units, so it bridges prototype and production

Limits

  • Still an insert per design, so a revision is new tooling
  • Surface replication only: no undercuts, no internal voids, and every part needs a separate bonding step, as with PDMS
  • High aspect ratio features are damaged on demold and need generous draft angles

Thermoplastic injection molding

Melted polymer injected into a hardened steel tool. The route a device takes once the design is frozen and the volume justifies the tooling.

Strengths

  • Genuine production scale, with parts produced in seconds
  • Very low cost per part once the tool exists
  • Materials such as COC, cyclic olefin copolymer, absorb far less than PDMS, which suits assay work
  • Repeatable enough for regulated manufacturing

Limits

  • Tooling is priced per design and a revision means a new block of steel
  • Locks the design before it has been validated, which inverts the research order
  • Weeks of lead time per iteration
  • Cannot produce enclosed or undercut three dimensional geometry in one part

Glass and silicon

Etched substrates using semiconductor fabrication. The route for chemistry, pressure, or optical requirements that polymers cannot meet.

Strengths

  • Chemical and thermal resistance beyond any polymer
  • The finest feature resolution available
  • No absorption of small molecules, and optically stable

Limits

  • Requires semiconductor facility access, which few labs have
  • Weeks per iteration
  • Cost per chip stays high at every volume
  • Brittle, and difficult to integrate with soft components

Resin printing with the Aionik™ cure

Print the part on standard SLA hardware, then run our patent pending post process in place of the chemical wash. No master, no mold, no cleanroom for the part itself.

Strengths

  • No master mold, so no cleanroom step and no tooling to amortize
  • A revision is a file edit, not a new tool, so the design loop closes in an afternoon
  • Cure runs 1 to 3 minutes for current production and does not exceed 15, whatever the part
  • True three dimensional geometry, including enclosed volumes that no mold based route can demold
  • Optical clarity, smooth surfaces, and biocompatibility from general purpose resins
  • The same process at prototype and at production volume
  • A post process, so it composes with whatever the printer can resolve (how that works, and which printers apply)

Limits

  • Feature resolution is set by the printer, so the finest photolithographic geometry is still lithography's domain
  • Resin viscosity is a hard ceiling. Bottom up SLA and DLP work reliably up to roughly 2,000 to 3,000 mPa·s, and thicker proprietary formulations have given us poor prints. Most resins we have run work; a few have not
  • Solvent compatibility follows the resin, not the process. Printed resins swell in DMSO, THF and toluene, which changes channel dimensions, though water, alcohols and nonpolar solvents are unaffected
  • Printed resin is not gas permeable the way PDMS is, which matters for cell culture that depends on oxygen exchange through the device wall
  • Autofluorescence can run slightly above PDMS, which is among the lowest of any polymer, and that matters for some fluorescence imaging
  • The full limits are not mapped. These are the ones we have hit or found published; more work is needed to know where the rest are
The pattern

Every route is good at iterating or at producing, not both

These routes are stages, not rivals

In practice a lab does not choose one of these. It uses several in sequence: prototype in PDMS because iteration is possible there, then move to hot embossing or injection molding when volume is needed. The routes depend on each other, and that dependency is the expensive part. The handoff costs a redesign for a new process, fresh tooling, and revalidation of a device that is no longer quite the device that was tested. Our process is meant to remove the handoff rather than any one of these routes, by holding the same material and the same process on both sides of it.

If you own none of it, you wait

Most labs own none of these processes, so they send the design to a contract manufacturer and buy finished chips. That is a sourcing decision rather than a fabrication method, which is why it is not in the matrix: the foundry runs one of the routes above on your behalf, and you inherit its limits along with its capabilities.

What you also inherit is the queue. Buying custom devices takes one to twelve weeks depending on the vendor and the complexity of the design, and every revision starts that clock again. Setup and tooling are charged per design, so cost moves with how unusual your geometry is and supply moves with how busy the vendor is. Researchers routinely wait months to hold a design they drew in an afternoon. The wait, not the invoice, is what actually limits how many questions a lab can ask in a year.

That split is the reason a device gets validated in one material and shipped in another. It is a structural feature of how these processes work, not a failure of anyone using them, and it is the specific gap our process is built to close.

Evidence and hypothesis

What is demonstrated, and what is hypothesis

Some of what we believe about this process is demonstrated and some of it is reasoning we have not yet tested. Which is which, stated per item.

01

Hypothesis: finer printers, same cure

Ours is a post process, so in principle it applies to any resin printed part, including output from the highest resolution microfluidic printers on the market. If that holds, the finest printed geometry becomes curable the same way. We are testing it now, though not at the rate we could, and it is one of the things we need capacity for.

02

Lithography still owns the finest features

Today, where a design needs geometry finer than a printer resolves, photolithography and silicon remain the right tools. The hypothesis above is how that boundary could move, not a claim that it already has.

03

Independent validation is a need, not a result

Our results are our own. Third party test data with methodology is one of the things we are seeking, and it is listed among what we need on the homepage.

04

Lab on a chip is adjacent, not addressed

Integrated devices such as electrode bearing organoid chips look like a geometry and materials problem rather than new chemistry, which is why we want to test the process against them. We have built none, we claim nothing here, and the access to try is among what we need.

05

Absorption is theory, not measurement

Material class suggests a glassy printed resin takes up far less than an elastomer such as PDMS. That comparison is underway and is another of the things we need capacity for. We do not present our own results as validation.
