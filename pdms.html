The incumbent process

How a PDMS chip is actually made

Soft lithography is the standard way to make a microfluidic chip, and it has been for two decades. It is also slow, expensive, and unforgiving. This page walks the whole process, from bare silicon wafer to a bonded device, and is honest about where it goes wrong.

Step by step

Eleven steps, and most of a week

The process splits in two. First you build a mold, which is the part that needs the cleanroom. Then you cast against it, which is the part that needs the hands. A mold can be reused, so the first half is amortized. The second half is repeated for every single chip.

Phase one

Building the master mold

Cleanroom required. Done once per design.
1

Clean the wafer

A bare silicon wafer is solvent cleaned and dehydration baked so the photoresist will adhere.

Any particle left behind becomes a defect reproduced into every chip cast from this mold.
2

Spin coat the photoresist

SU-8 is poured onto the wafer centre and spun to depth. Spin speed sets channel height, so the recipe is the geometry.

Edge bead, bubbles, or a speed error changes channel height across the whole wafer.
3

Soft bake

Baked in stages on a hotplate, typically around 65 °C then 95 °C, to drive off solvent before exposure.

Under baking leaves solvent and the resist sticks to the mask. Over baking cracks it.
4

Align and expose

The photomask is aligned to the wafer in a mask aligner and the pattern is written with UV.

Alignment is done by an operator through optics. Dose errors under or over cure the features.
5

Develop and silanize

Uncured resist is dissolved away, leaving raised channel features. The mold is then vapour silanized so cured rubber will release from it.

Skip or rush silanization and the first casting tears the mold apart, taking the design with it.
Phase two

Casting and finishing the chip

Repeated for every chip. Mostly by hand.
6

Mix the PDMS

Base and curing agent are mixed, conventionally around ten to one. The ratio sets stiffness and how well it will bond later.

Mixing traps air throughout the batch, which is why the next step exists.
7

Degas

The mixture sits under vacuum until the bubbles stirred in during mixing rise out and collapse.

A bubble that survives into a channel wall is a scrapped chip, found only after curing.
8

Pour and cure

Poured over the mold and cured with heat, commonly around 60 °C for several hours or overnight.

This is the wait. The design is frozen for the length of the cure before anyone learns whether it worked.
9

Cut and peel

The cured slab is cut out and peeled off the mold by hand.

The most physical step. Peel wrong and you tear the slab, damage fine features, or crack the wafer underneath.
10

Punch the ports

Every inlet and outlet is punched individually with a biopsy punch, positioned by eye against the channel end.

A punch that misses, tears, or leaves a plug of debris in the channel ruins the chip after all prior work.
11

Activate and bond

The slab and a glass slide are plasma treated, then pressed together to form a permanent seal.

One attempt. Contact is irreversible, and the activation is already decaying (see below).
Elapsed time

From an idea to holding one

Cost is the number people quote. Time is the one that actually shapes the research, because it sets how many times a question can be asked and answered in a year.

The top two rows of that chart describe labs that can afford a cleanroom. Most cannot. The equipment and the room together are a capital commitment few research groups can justify for a fabrication step that is not itself the research, so the work goes out to a foundry, and the group joins the bottom row.

That is the part worth sitting with. A researcher with an idea on Monday is not asking when the chip will be finished. They are asking whether the question is worth a purchase order and a month. Ideas that would take an afternoon to test never get tested, because the fabrication step prices them out of existence.

And it compounds with iteration. A first design is rarely right. The second version means a new photomask, a new master mold, or a new order, and the same wait again. A group that could run ten iterations a year on a fast loop runs two or three on a slow one, and the difference is not effort or funding. It is fabrication turnaround.

The human factor

People call it an art, and they are not being poetic

Eleven steps, and roughly half of them depend on an individual's hands and judgement rather than on a setting you can dial in. How fast to peel. When the degassing is finished. Where exactly to place the punch. How much pressure to apply as two surfaces meet, once, permanently.

The consequence is that outcomes track the operator. A lab with someone experienced produces working chips. The same protocol, same equipment, and a new graduate student produces a stack of scrap while they learn the feel of it. That knowledge lives in people, not in documentation, which is why the capability concentrates in a small number of institutions and why it does not transfer easily between them.

It also shows up in the literature. Even the standard test for whether a bond held is a manual peel, which yields no quantitative data and is acknowledged to be subjective with poor reproducibility. The field's own quality check is a person pulling on a chip to see what happens.

Failure modes

Where it goes wrong

Most of these are only discovered at the end, after every prior step has already been paid for in time and material.

Delamination at the seam

The classic failure. A bond fails when a path of separation can be traced from the channel out to the edge of the device. Under pressure, the interface is what gives.

Inlet pin ejection

Tubing pushed into a hand punched port pops back out under flow, because the port was punched slightly oversized or off axis.

Bulk splitting

The rubber itself tears rather than the bond, which is what happens when the mix ratio was adjusted to favour bond strength at the cost of material strength.

Trapped bubbles

Air that survived degassing sits in a channel wall. Invisible until the slab is cured and cut, at which point the chip is scrap.

Tearing on demold

Peeling a cured slab off the master is done by hand. Fine or high aspect ratio features are the first to go, and a badly stuck slab can take the mold with it.

The window closing

Alignment takes longer than expected, the activated surface decays, and the bond that results is weak in a way that only shows up later under flow.

Those first three are documented failure classes in the bonding literature, not anecdotes: visible delamination, inlet pin ejection, and bulk PDMS splitting are the named modes researchers sort their broken devices into.

Service life

A chip is two pieces pretending to be one

There is a second, quieter version of the same problem. The plasma treatment that makes bonding possible also makes the surface temporarily water friendly, and that property does not last either. The same hydrophobic recovery that closes the bonding window keeps going after the device is sealed, so the internal surface a protocol was validated against is not necessarily the surface it has weeks later.

Neither issue is exotic or a sign of a bad lab. They are properties of building a channel by sealing two parts together, in a material chosen for how easily it casts.

The other route: injection molding

Soft lithography is how chips get prototyped. Injection molding is how they get produced. It solves the throughput problem and creates a different one, because the geometry stops being a file and becomes a piece of machined steel.

Tooling figures as published by Meridian Medical and Formlabs. The number that matters is not the first tool, it is that you buy another one for every design change. A revision is not a file edit, it is a new block of steel and the weeks of machining that go with it. That is why labs prototype in PDMS and manufacture in thermoplastic, and why the device that gets validated is not the device that ships. Medical grade polymers and cleanroom molding are priced on top.

Individual features are priced separately on top: pass throughs, air showers, and gowning rooms add $5,000 to $25,000 each. None of it includes the recurring bill, which is energy, filter replacement, preventive maintenance, and revalidation for every year the room stays open.

Add it up and the pattern is the same at every scale. The equipment is expensive, the room is expensive, and both are fixed costs that have to be carried whether or not anyone is producing chips that week. Underneath all of it sits the operator, who has to be trained, retained, and paid, and without whom none of the above produces a working device.

The point

None of this is anyone's fault

Soft lithography is a good process that has carried the field a long way. It is simply a process built around a mold, a cleanroom, and a pair of trained hands, and every one of those is a constraint on how fast a lab can go from an idea to a part. We took out the step that forced the tradeoff.
